1. Executive Summary & Outage Background

1.1 Incident Overview

Sudsline Artisan Soap Manufacturing operates a distributed enterprise infrastructure encompassing three distinct functional facilities:

  • Head Office (HQ - Design & Sales Showroom): Houses corporate management, product design, and sales operations. Acts as the enterprise network core, hosting dual-homed eBGP Internet edge routing (AS 65000) peering with Primary ISP (AS 65001) and Secondary ISP (AS 65002), as well as the Area 0 OSPF backbone routing engine.
  • Curing & Batching Plant: The primary operational technology (OT) facility responsible for soap saponification, batch mixing, and temperature-controlled curing. Runs a legacy EIGRP (AS 100) network integrated into the corporate backbone. Contains mission-critical batch-monitoring terminals that track sensitive chemical processes.
  • Packaging Warehouse: The shipping, inventory management, and barcode scanning depot. Connected to the Batching Plant via redundant primary (Gigabit) and secondary (Serial) WAN circuits running EIGRP, and backhauled to HQ via OSPF Area 0.

Over the preceding 48 hours following a scheduled network maintenance window and equipment re-cabling, staff at all three facilities experienced cascading communication failures. These included recurring batch telemetry blackouts, broken inter-site communications, missing B2B partner links, and degraded inventory synchronization. The on-call engineering team systematically isolated five symptoms (labeled A through E).

4. In-Depth Root-Cause Diagnostics (Objectives 1–5)

4.1 Symptom A: Layer 2 Spanning Tree Instability & TCN Storm (Objective 1)

Observed Behavior: Batch-monitoring terminals lose connectivity for a few seconds every 10–15 minutes. show spanning-tree shows one access port repeatedly toggling between blocking and forwarding, and topology-change notifications (TCNs) are far above baseline.

Root Cause Identification:

The most likely root cause is a rogue or unmanaged switch (or unmanaged hub/bridged device) connected to access port Fa0/10 that introduces a redundant loop path and injects Bridge Protocol Data Units (BPDUs) into the switched domain.

Technical Mechanism: In default Spanning Tree Protocol (IEEE 802.1D / PVST+), when an unmanaged switch is plugged into an access port that lacks spanning-tree portfast and spanning-tree bpduguard, any link state change or broadcast loop on that rogue device causes BPDUs to be transmitted into the corporate switch. When the switch receives BPDUs or detects an active loop, the port transitions through Blocking → Listening (15s) → Learning (15s) → Forwarding.

Each time this transition occurs, the switch generates a Topology Change Notification (TCN) toward the root bridge (Plant-Core-SW). Upon receiving a TCN, the root bridge broadcasts a Topology Change (TC) flag in configuration BPDUs across VLAN 20. This forces all switches in the VLAN to reduce their MAC address table (CAM) aging timer from the standard 300 seconds down to the Forward Delay timer (15 seconds).

Consequently, active CAM entries for the batch-monitoring terminals (Batch-Terminal-1 and Batch-Terminal-2) are prematurely flushed every 10–15 minutes. The switch is forced to flood unicast frames to all ports as unknown unicast flooding while relearning MAC addresses, causing temporary buffer starvation, port state blocking, and 5–15 second telemetry loss for batch monitoring.

4.2 Symptom B: OSPF Adjacency Stall in EXSTART/EXCHANGE (Objective 2)

Observed Behavior: show ip ospf neighbor shows the neighbor stuck in EXSTART/EXCHANGE and never reaches FULL, even though the link is up and pings succeed.

Why OSPF Neighbors Reach 2-WAY but Stall Before FULL:

OSPF adjacency formation proceeds through distinct finite state machine (FSM) states:

  1. DOWN → INIT → 2-WAY: Neighbors exchange small OSPF Hello packets (multicast 224.0.0.5). In the Hello packet, neighbors verify common Area ID, subnet mask, hello/dead intervals, authentication, and stub flags. Upon seeing its own Router ID in the neighbor's Hello, the router transitions to 2-WAY. On broadcast or multi-access networks, DR/BDR election occurs here. Crucially, Hello packets do not negotiate or validate interface MTU size.
  2. EXSTART → EXCHANGE: In the EXSTART state, neighbors elect Master/Slave roles using Database Description (DBD) packets with the Init, More, and Master (I/M/MS) bits set. In the EXCHANGE state, the Master and Slave exchange DBD packets containing Link-State Database (LSDB) summary headers.

The Specific Mismatch Responsible: Interface MTU Mismatch

According to RFC 2328 (OSPF Version 2, Section 10.6), every OSPF DBD packet header contains the Interface MTU field, which represents the largest IP datagram (in bytes) that can be sent without fragmentation on that interface.

If HQ-Edge-Router has an MTU of 1500 bytes on Gi0/1, while Plant-Router has an MTU of 1400 bytes (introduced during WAN re-cabling or MTU testing):

  • When HQ-Edge-Router transmits a DBD packet advertising Interface MTU = 1500, Plant-Router inspects the field. Because 1500 > 1400, Plant-Router determines it cannot receive datagrams of that size and silently drops the DBD packet.
  • HQ-Edge-Router never receives an acknowledgment for its DBD packet. After the retransmission timer (typically 5 seconds) expires, it retransmits the DBD.
  • Eventually, the adjacency resets back to EXSTART, cycles to EXCHANGE, and fails again, remaining indefinitely stuck in EXSTART/EXCHANGE.

Why ICMP Pings Succeed: Standard Cisco IOS pings send ICMP Echo Request packets with a default payload of only 100 bytes (56/64 bytes on Linux/Windows). Since 100 bytes is far below both 1400 and 1500, pings traverse the link cleanly without fragmentation, giving the misleading impression that the IP link is entirely healthy.

4.3 Symptom C: EIGRP Route Flapping & Feasibility Condition (Objective 3)

Observed Behavior: The successor route to the Packaging Warehouse subnet flaps in and out of the routing table every few minutes; show interfaces shows a much lower bandwidth value on one link than the others in that path.

EIGRP Composite Metric Reaction:

EIGRP calculates the composite path metric using the standard formula (with default K-values: K1=1, K2=0, K3=1, K4=0, K5=0):

Metric = 256 * [ (10^7 / Minimum Bandwidth in kbps) + (Sum of Delays in tens of microseconds / 10) ]

Notice that the metric is inversely proportional to the minimum bandwidth along the entire path. On the dual links between Plant-Router and WH-Router:

  • Primary Path (Gi0/2): Bandwidth is 1,000,000 kbps (1 Gbps). The resulting Feasible Distance (FD) is very small: FD_primary = 256 * [ (10^7 / 1000000) + (10 + 10)/10 ] = 256 * (10 + 2) = 3072.
  • Secondary Path (Se0/0/0): If the interface was erroneously configured with a depressed bandwidth value (e.g. legacy serial bandwidth of 64 kbps or 1544 kbps): Metric_secondary = 256 * [ (10^7 / 64) + (20000 + 10)/10 ] ≈ 40,514,816.

Why Route Flapping Occurs Rather Than a Clean Failover:

For EIGRP to perform an instantaneous, sub-second failover, the alternate path MUST satisfy the Feasibility Condition (FC):

Reported Distance (RD) < Feasible Distance (FD) of current successor

The Reported Distance (RD / Advertised Distance) is the metric calculated by the neighboring router for that destination. When the bandwidth discrepancy or delay on the backup link is severe:

  1. The secondary link's RD fails the Feasibility Condition (RD ≥ FD). As a result, EIGRP refuses to install the secondary path as a Feasible Successor (FS) in the topology table.
  2. When transient micro-congestion, delay spikes, or keepalive drops affect the primary link, Plant-Router loses its successor route. Because there is NO Feasible Successor in the topology table, the route immediately transitions into the ACTIVE state.
  3. While ACTIVE, the route is withdrawn from the IP routing table. Plant-Router must broadcast EIGRP Query packets across all neighbors, wait for Query Replies, recalculate the metric from scratch, and reinstall the route.
  4. When the primary link recovers seconds later, EIGRP preempts back to the primary link, causing the route to flap in and out of the routing table continuously every few minutes.

4.4 Symptom D: eBGP Session Established but Partner Prefix Missing (Objective 4)

Observed Behavior: The eBGP session to the secondary ISP shows 'Established', but a specific partner route that should be received is missing from show ip bgp, and traceroutes toward it dead-end at the ISP.

BGP Peering State vs. Route Propagation:

A BGP session status of 'Established' only confirms the successful completion of the underlying TCP three-way handshake (port 179) and the mutual exchange of BGP OPEN and initial KEEPALIVE messages. During this phase, peers negotiate:

  • Autonomous System (AS) numbers
  • BGP Version (Version 4)
  • BGP Hold Time and Keepalive timers
  • BGP Multiprotocol Capabilities

Once Established, prefix exchange occurs asynchronously via BGP UPDATE messages. An Established session does NOT imply that any routing prefixes have been advertised, received, or accepted into the BGP Routing Information Base (BGP RIB).

Why the Prefix is Missing from show ip bgp:

In BGP, if a prefix is missing completely from show ip bgp (not even marked with * for valid or r for RIB failure), the route was never installed into the local BGP Loc-RIB. The standard 10-step BGP Best-Path Selection algorithm (Weight → Local Preference → Locally Originated → AS Path → Origin Code → MED → eBGP over iBGP → IGP Metric → Router ID) was never even invoked because the prefix was discarded upon ingress or never sent!

Two Critical Configuration Points to Inspect First:

  1. Point 1: Inbound Route Filtering on HQ-Edge-Router (Customer Side):
    Inspect the inbound route-map, prefix-list, or distribute-list bound to the secondary ISP neighbor:
    HQ-Edge-Router# show running-config | section router bgp router bgp 65000 neighbor 198.51.100.1 remote-as 65002 neighbor 198.51.100.1 route-map RM-ISP2-IN in HQ-Edge-Router# show ip prefix-list ip prefix-list PARTNER-PREFIX: 1 entries seq 5 permit 0.0.0.0/0 [Missing permit for 198.18.50.0/24 - dropped by implicit deny!]
    If the prefix-list permits default route 0.0.0.0/0 but lacks a permit statement for 198.18.50.0/24, Cisco IOS will silently discard the partner route upon ingress due to the default implicit deny all.
  2. Point 2: Outbound Route Origination & Export Policy on Secondary ISP (Provider Side):
    Inspect whether ISP2 is actually originating the partner route or filtering it outbound:
    ISP2-Secondary# show ip bgp neighbors 198.51.100.2 advertised-routes
    If ISP2 lacks the network 198.18.50.0 mask 255.255.255.0 command, or if the prefix does not exist in ISP2's global IP routing table, ISP2 will never generate an UPDATE message for that prefix.

4.5 Symptom E: Wireshark Gratuitous ARP Pattern & Switch Defense (Objective 5)

Observed Behavior: A packet capture from a Batching Plant access port shows an unusually high rate of gratuitous ARP replies, all claiming the default-gateway IP address but originating from several different MAC addresses.

Pattern Indication & Attack Identification:

This capture signature is the definitive hallmark of an ARP Spoofing / ARP Cache Poisoning Attack (executed via tools such as Ettercap, Arpspoof, or Scapy).

Mechanics: Address Resolution Protocol (ARP) is inherently stateless and unauthenticated; hosts and routers accept unsolicited (gratuitous) ARP replies and unconditionally overwrite their dynamic ARP cache tables. The rogue host (Rogue-Attacker-PC on Fa0/3) floods the local broadcast domain with gratuitous ARP (GARP) replies asserting:

"Sender IP: 10.20.10.1 (Default Gateway) ↔ Sender MAC: <Attacker MAC / Spoofed MACs>"

When legitimate industrial terminals (Batch-Terminal-1 and Batch-Terminal-2) process these poisoned frames, their ARP tables map the plant gateway IP 10.20.10.1 to the attacker's physical hardware address.

Likely Attacker Intent:

  • Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) Eavesdropping: Intercept and inspect industrial telemetry, recipe proportions, saponification monitoring logs, and proprietary chemical soap formulas transmitted between batch terminals and plant servers.
  • Industrial Sabotage / Telemetry Tampering: Alter chemical batch control parameters in-flight before relaying packets to the true gateway, potentially ruining soap curing cycles.
  • Denial of Service (DoS): Blackholing plant production traffic by capturing and discarding outbound gateway frames, causing operational halt.

Two Concrete Switch-Level Mitigations:

  1. Mitigation 1: Dynamic ARP Inspection (DAI) (Primary Mitigation):
    DAI intercepts all ARP requests and replies on untrusted switchports. It validates the sender MAC and sender IP binding against the trusted DHCP Snooping Binding Database. If an ARP reply arrives on an untrusted port with an IP-to-MAC mapping not present in the database (or claiming the gateway IP), the switch instantly discards the frame and logs a security violation.
  2. Mitigation 2: DHCP Snooping (Prerequisite & Rogue Server Defense):
    DHCP Snooping categorizes switchports into trusted (uplinks to legitimate DHCP servers/routers) and untrusted (all end-user access ports). It filters DHCP offer/ack messages from unauthorized devices and dynamically builds the IP-to-MAC-to-Switchport binding database that DAI relies upon.
  3. Supplementary Mitigation: Port Security:
    Restricts the number of valid MAC addresses on each access port to 1 (switchport port-security maximum 1) and enforces violation restrict or shutdown, preventing rogue attackers from forging multiple source MAC addresses on a single physical link.

5. Holistic Synthesis & Correlation Analysis (Objective 6)

Investigation Narrative: Are the Five Symptoms Unrelated or Shared Triggers?
Written Narrative Length: 395 words. Technical Synthesis of 48-Hour Incident Log.

A rigorous holistic analysis of the 48-hour incident log indicates that the five symptoms are not five isolated anomalies. Rather, they represent a composite failure scenario driven by two distinct yet interconnected catalysts: an active physical and cyber intrusion event at the Batching Plant, and latent configuration drift exposed by a recent network maintenance window.

The most conclusive correlation exists between Symptom A (STP port flapping and TCN generation) and Symptom E (high-volume gratuitous ARP flood). Both events converged simultaneously on the exact same physical layer: the access switch of the Curing & Batching Plant (Plant-Access-SW). The evidence proves that an unauthorized actor—or an employee attempting an unauthorized bypass—introduced an unmanaged switch or multi-homed laptop into the plant floor access layer. The rogue device lacked spanning-tree protection, causing port toggling and high TCNs whenever looped or rebooted (Symptom A). Concurrently, this exact device was used as the staging platform for an ARP cache poisoning attack against the default gateway (10.20.10.1) using multiple spoofed MAC addresses (Symptom E). Thus, Symptoms A and E share an undeniable single root trigger: an untrusted rogue hardware asset plugged into an unhardened access switchport.

Meanwhile, Symptoms B, C, and D share a secondary common trigger: configuration drift and protocol integration errors introduced during the recent 48-hour WAN expansion. To connect the legacy EIGRP plant and the Packaging Warehouse to the new OSPF backbone and dual-ISP BGP edge:

  • Engineers provisioned the inter-site WAN link (Gi0/1) but neglected to standardize the MTU, leaving Plant-Router at 1400 bytes while HQ-Edge-Router was at 1500 bytes. This stalled OSPF in EXSTART/EXCHANGE (Symptom B).
  • During the same maintenance window, re-cabling between the Batching Plant and Warehouse left the backup serial WAN interface with an inaccurate bandwidth configuration (64 kbps vs 1000 Mbps), breaking EIGRP's Feasibility Condition and turning link failover into a flapping query cycle (Symptom C).
  • Simultaneously, the secondary ISP eBGP session was brought online at HQ, but the inbound prefix-list was configured with an overly restrictive filter permitting only 0.0.0.0/0, silently dropping the critical partner route 198.18.50.0/24 (Symptom D).

In conclusion, the incident was triggered by a rogue device insertion on the plant floor executing ARP spoofing while inducing Layer 2 STP flaps, compounded by a lack of change-control validation across MTU, EIGRP bandwidth, and BGP route filters during recent inter-site integration.
